%------------------------- Problem 1 -----------------------

\begin{problem}
	\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{1.png}
	\hfill
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Suppose for contradiction that a Riemann map $\Delta \to \mathcal C$
could not be extended continuously to the boundary. That is, we would
have two sequences in $\Delta$ converging to some point on the
boundary, $z_n, z_n' \to z_0 \in S^1$, such that their images
converged to distinct points on $\partial \mathcal C$. 
In other words, there is some positive $\delta$ such that $\abs{f(z_n)
- f(z_m')} > \delta$ for all $n, m$.

Consider the following series of calculation. 
Consider circles $\alpha_r$ of radius $r$ about $z_0$. For sufficiently large
$n,m$ and sufficiently small $r$ there are points in both sequences
which lie on $\alpha_r$. Now, by the fundamental theorem of calculus
we have 
\begin{align*}
	\delta < \abs{f(z_n) - f(z_m')} &= \abs{ \int_{\alpha_r} f'(\xi) d\xi} \\
		&= \abs{\int_{\theta_1(r)}^{\theta_2(r)} f'(z_0 + re^{i \theta}) r d\theta} \\
		&\leq \abs{\int_{\theta_1(r)}^{\theta_2(r)}} \abs{f'(z_0 + re^{i\theta})} r d\theta
\end{align*}
where the second equality is given by parameterizing $\alpha_r$ and
the final inequality given by the triangle inequality; the
angles $\theta_1(r), \theta_2(r)$ are the angles on which $z_n, z_m'$ respectively
lie on $\alpha_r$.
Now, squaring both sides and then applying Cauchy-Schwarz (taking 
$h = f r^{1/2}$, $g = r^{1/2}$) gives
\begin{align*}
	\delta^2 
	&\leq \int \abs{f'(z)}^2 r d\theta \int_{\theta_1(r)}^{\theta_2(r)} r d\theta \\
	&\leq \int \abs{f'(z)}^2 r d\theta \int_{0}^{2\pi} r d\theta  \\
	&\leq \int \abs{f'(z)}^2 r d\theta \left(2\pi r\right).
\end{align*}
Rearranging this inequality and then integrating over $r$ gives 
\[
	\int_{r=0}^{R} \frac{\delta^2}{r} dr 
	\leq \int_{r} \int_{\theta} \abs{f'(z)}^2 r d\theta dr = \int\int \abs{f'}^2  dA = \text{Area}(f(\Delta)),
\]
where the last equality is given by the fact that the Jacobian for the
appropriate transformation is $\abs{f'(z)}^2$. 
Now finally, notice that the left hand side diverges, but the right
hand side is finite, since it is the area of a bounded open set. A
contradiction, and so it must have been that $f$ extended continuously
to the boundary.

As a final note, notice that the hypothesis that the boundary be
homeomorphic to the circle means that our boundary cannot have any
self intersections. I.e. we have a ``nice boundary'', and the
situation really is like the polygon case.

\end{solution}

%------------------------- Problem 2 -----------------------

\begin{problem}
	\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{2.png}
	\hfill
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Recall that we have a biholomorphism from the $\Delta \to \H$ given by 
\[
	h(z) = i \cdot \frac{1 + z}{1 - z}.
\]
This map extends continuously to the boudaries $\bar \Delta \to \Hbdy$
with $h(1) = \infty$. 
And so we can define a holomorphic function $g: \H \to \H$ by 
$g = h \circ f \circ h\inv$ which extends continuously to the actual
boundary $\bar \H \cup \infty$ and $g(\bar \H \cup \infty) \subseteq
\bar \H \cup \infty$.
The preimages of $1$ under $f$ are those which get sent to infinity
under $g$. 

Suppose for a moment that we have a single point on the real line
which gets sent to infinity. Say that we translate our coordinates so
that this point is $z=0$. Let us denote the ``right half upper plane''
by $\H^+ := \{z \in \H : Re(z) > 0\}$ and similarly let $\H^-$ denote
the ``left upper half plane''.

Now, the restriction of $g$ to $\H^+$ defines a holomorphic function
which extends continuously to the interval $(0, \infty)$ with the
image of $(0, \infty) \subseteq \R$. 
The Schwarz reflection principle then allows us to extend
$g\rest{\H^+}$ to a holomorphic function on the entire right half
plane $\R^+ := \{z \in \C: Re(z) > 0 \}$. The same argument means that
we can extend $g\rest{\H^-}$ to a holomorphic function on the entire left half plane.
We now have a holomorphic function $\C \setminus L \to \C$ where $L$
is the vertical line through $z=0$. Let us denote this function by
$\tilde g$. 

\begin{center}
	\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=2]

  % Axes
  \draw[->] (-2,0) -- (2,0) node[right] {$Re(z)$};
  %\draw[->] (0,-2) -- (0,2) node[above] {$\Im(z)$};

  % Dotted vertical line at z = 0
  \draw[dotted, thick] (0,-2) -- (0,2);

  % Labels for regions
  \node at (1,1) {$\mathbb{H}^+$};
  \node at (-1,1) {$\mathbb{H}^-$};
  \node at (0.3,-0.2) {$z=0$};
  \fill (0,0) circle (1pt);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

We can extend $\tilde g$ further to a function $G: \C \setminus
\{z=0\} \to \C$ in the following way
\[
	G(z) = \begin{cases}
		\tilde g(z) & z \not\in L \\
		g(z) & z \in L \cap \H \\
		\overline{g(\bar z)} & z \in L \cap -\H.
	\end{cases}
\]
The proof that Scharz reflection gives a holomorphic function also
proves that this function is holomorphic as a function $\C \setminus
\{z = 0 \} \to \C$. 

We can translate $G$ into a holomorphic extension of $f$ by
conjugating $F := h\inv \circ G \circ h : \C \setminus \{p\} \to \C$ where
$p$ is the preimage of $1$ under the continuous extension $\overline
f$.

If $f$ has more than one point on $S^1$ which is sent to $1$ (i.e.
more than one point on $\R$ is sent to $\infty$ under $h$) then we can
repeat a similar procedure, except now we Schwarz reflect vertical
strips bounded by the vertical lines through the points on the real
line which are sent to $\infty$ by $h$. 

Now notice that our extended function $F$ may have poles. 
In particular, the Riemann map $h\inv: \H \to \Delta$
is given by
\[
	w \mapsto \frac{w - i}{w+i}
\]
which has a pole whenever $w = -i$. That is to say, any $z \in \C
\setminus \{p\}$ such that $G(z) = -i$ corresponds to a pole on the
``unit disk'' side. 
Now, recall that $g$ mapped $\H \to \H$ since $f$ mapped $\Delta \to
\Delta$. And so the only points which have image $-i$ under $G$ are
those in the lower half plane. That is, consider the points in the
lower half plane such that $\overline{g(\bar z)} = -i$ or the points
in the upper half plane such that $g(z) = i$. Recalling the
biholomorphism $\Delta \to \H$, these are exactly the points $z \in
\Delta$ such that $f(z) = 0$. I.e. the reflected zeroes of $f$ become
poles in the extended domain.
Lastly, since $f$ has isolated zeroes (it is holomorphic), and 
 since $\Delta$ is a compact set, 
$f$ can only have finitely many zeros --- any discrete set contained
in a compact set is necessarily finite.
And so the extended function only has finitely many poles.

Thus we have found an extended function of $f$ which is holomorphic on
all of $\C$ with only finitely many points removed.










\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
	\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{3.png}
	\hfill
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
I think the idea with this question is that we can apply a
``reflection principle'' to this set with these conditions on
$f$ (similar to how we used inversion to apply a reflection principle
to the unit disc in the previous question).

More precisely, the Riemann mapping theorem gives us that there's a
biholomorphism from $U$ to either the upper half plane or the unit
disc. Indeed, $U$ is simply connected and not all of $\C$. 
Moreover, since the boundary of $U$ is ``sufficiently nice'',
this Riemann map extends to a homeomorphism on the boundary. 
Then, we can define a holomorphic function on the upper half plane, by
conjugating our original $f$ with the Riemann map, and this new
function satisfies the conditions for Schwarz reflection.
This gives us a new holomorphic function defined on an open set strictly larger
than the upper
half plane. 
We can map this larger set back to original domain with
the parabola using our Riemann map again. And then our desired
extended function is given
by conjugating the extended function on the ``upper half plane side''
by our Riemann map. 

There are some details with poles which need to be thought about. 
We may have a nonfinite number of poles in our extension, since the parabola is not a
compact set. However, they need to be isolated, and so we can remove
any poles found in our extension and still be left with a strictly
larger open set than the original parabola on which our new function
is defined on.
\end{solution}

%------------------------- Problem 5 -----------------------

\begin{problem}
	\includegraphics[scale=0.8]{5.png}
	\hfill
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
First recall that we have a well defined logarithm on the upper half
plane (take the branch cut on any ray contained in the lower half
plane) and so we can write $\log z$ suggestively as 
\[
	\log z = \int_0^z \frac{1}{\xi} \, d\xi.
\]
On the other hand, writing $\log z = \log(r e^{i\theta}) = \ln r +
i\theta$, and remembering that points on the upper half plane have $0 <
\theta < \pi$, the image $\ln(\H)$ is the infinite horizontal strip
given by $\{z \in \C: 0 < Im(z) < \pi \}$. This ``looks'' like a
polygon whose corners are occuring off at infinity.

More precisely, any radial ray from $z=0$ at some angle $\theta$ is
mapped to a horizontal line (``moving to the right'') at $im(z) =
\theta$. Consider the image of the boundary rays under $\log z$. We
have that the ray $(0, \infty)$ is mapped to the ``lower''
boundary component $im(z) = 0$. On the other hand, the ray
$(0,-\infty)$ is mapped to the ``upper'' boundary component at $im(z) =
\pi$. 

If we want to think of this map as a degenerate
Scharz-Christoffel map, then we might want to think of what a
``continuous extension'' of $\log z$ to the boundary might mean (of
course, this is a heuristic argument since $\log z$ is ill defined at
$z= 0$).
Approaching $z=
0$ from $+\infty$ gives a point ``infinitely far to the left'' on the
line in the image $im(z) = 0$ whereas approaching $z=0$ from $-\infty$ gives a
point also ``infinitely far to the left'' but now on the line $im(z) =
\pi$. If we want to think about a continuous extension, we can then
think of these two boundary lines as meeting at infinity. Then we can
think of this infinite strip as having a ``corner'' at $z = \infty$ in
the image, or at $z = 0$ on the boundary of the upper half plane. And
indeed, this is manifest in $\log z = \int_0^z 1/\xi d\xi$ since the
corners in Schwarz-Christoffel maps are supposed to occur at the images
of the denominator points. 
That is all to say, we can think of the image of $\log z$ applied to
the upper half plane as a degenerate polygon, with one corner at $z =
\infty$. 
If we want to extend our source domain to include the point at
infinity, then we can also think of the image of $\log(z)$ as having
another corner ``infinitely far to the right'', or at the image of
$\infty$.

\end{solution}
